Why this page leads with orders, not conversion rate

1 — August’s traffic was not real

August recorded 43,520 sessions against July’s 16,349. Almost all of the increase came from one place: Singapore went from a handful of sessions to 21,500, and desktop sessions rose 268%. By September it had collapsed back to 789.

Conversion rate is orders divided by sessions. Roughly 25,000 sessions that were never going to buy anything pushed August down to 0.68%. The store did not get five times worse in August, and it has not become five times better since. Anyone who quotes 0.68% to 3.96% at you, including me, is quoting a number with a hole in it.

2 — Shopify changed how it counts sessions

A notice on the analytics page reads: “Beginning Sept 21, 2026, sessions and related metrics may shift as Shopify Analytics enables better visibility into real shopper activity.” A Definition change marker sits on the September chart at that date.

So any conversion rate spanning 21 September is not a like-for-like comparison with one before it. Order counts are unaffected, which is why they are the headline on this page.

What I will and will not claim.

What I claim: completed orders went 234, 296, 353, 615 across four consecutive periods, carts rose about a third over the same stretch, and the share of carts that reached checkout went from half to almost all. Every one of those is on a screenshot on this page.

What I do not: that every order is down to me. Traffic mix and seasonality move month to month, Black Friday trading sits inside the last window, and no single change isolates a cause.

And what this page deliberately leaves out: revenue. The store’s sales figures are the client’s to publish, not mine, so you will not find a dollar amount anywhere on this site.

Project overview

A big catalogue that was hard to shop.

In July 2026 the store pulled 16,349 sessions and converted 234 of them. Only 5.5% of those visitors ever reached a cart.

That is not a traffic problem and it is not a pricing problem. It was a business losing orders inside its own catalogue, and then losing a second batch between the cart and the card.

Two separate leaks, two different fixes.

Before the cart. Only 5.5% got that far. People could not find the right product fast enough, and nothing told them why to trust the store with a card.

After the cart. Half of everyone at checkout left. In equipment retail that is usually shipping cost arriving as a surprise at the final step.

03

The shipping fix

Shipping stopped beinga surprise at the end.

Two changes aimed squarely at the second leak.

Shipping options were rebuilt around what customers actually buy. A rubber sheet and a full-size table are not the same parcel and should never have been offered the same way.

Then the threshold was made visible. A free shipping progress bar sits in the side cart and at checkout, counting in real money toward the $75 mark.

It does two jobs. Removes the late surprise that was killing checkouts, and gives a shopper a reason to add one more item rather than leave. Both states below, from the live store.

The goal was to turn the store into a structured, high-trust shopping experience while keeping the Shopify setup scalable and maintainable — so the client could keep running it without me.

What this means

The redesign did not just change how the site looks.

It changed how quickly people find a product, how much they trust the store, and whether shipping ambushes them at the last step. That is what a conversion rate is actually measuring.

And it stayed a Shopify store the client can run and extend themselves.
